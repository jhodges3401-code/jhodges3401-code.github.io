The math, in plain words

Junk removal pricing formula

Work out what one job really costs you, then divide by what's left of the price after your card fee and the profit you want to keep.

Price floor = (cost + card fixed fee) ÷ (1 − card fee % − target margin)
Suggested price = the floor rounded up to your price step, never below your minimum charge

Cost, line by line

  1. Load weight = cubic yards × your pounds per cubic yard for that material. (The spreadsheet can also add a known extra weight.)
  2. Dump fee = that weight in tons × what your dump charges per ton. A flat fee per load, if that's how your dump bills, replaces this.
  3. Special items = the fees you set for mattresses, TVs, appliances and anything else that costs extra to handle.
  4. Fuel = round-trip miles × your fuel cost per mile. Truck wear = the same miles × your wear cost per mile.
  5. Labor = crew size × hours on the job × hourly pay. Overhead is insurance, phone, software and the rest, spread over one job.

Without a card fee the formula is just cost ÷ (1 − target margin). With no margin at all, it gives the break-even price.

Worked example (made-up numbers)

The same half-truck job as the table below: 7.5 cubic yards, about 0.94 tons, a mattress and a TV, 21 miles, you plus one helper, paid by card. No spreadsheet file in this site lists the input rates, so the calculator uses the rates that reproduce that table to the cent.

  1. Weight: 7.5 × 250 lb = 1,875 lb = 0.9375 tons, shown as 0.94 tons.
  2. Dump: 0.9375 × $85 = $79.6875, which rounds to $79.69. Items $40.00. Fuel 21 × $0.327 = $6.87. Wear 21 × $0.25 = $5.25.
  3. Labor: 2 people × 2.625 hours × $21.50 = $112.88 for 5.25 labor hours. Overhead $12.00.
  4. Cost: those rounded lines add to $256.69.
  5. Floor: the cost before per-line rounding is $256.6795. ($256.6795 + $0.30) ÷ (1 − 0.029 − 0.35) = $413.82.
  6. Suggested: rounded up to the next $5 = $415.00. Card fee $12.34, profit $145.98, $49.31 per labor hour.

Per cubic yard, by the truck load, and what you still set

Price per cubic yard

Divide the job price by the cubic yards on that job. It is the same price seen another way, not a separate method. In the made-up example, $415.00 ÷ 7.5 cubic yards is $55.33 per cubic yard, and $415.00 ÷ 5.25 labor hours is $79.05 charged per labor hour. Neither carries over to a job with different weight, miles, stairs or dump fees, so price the next job from its own costs.

By truck load (quarter, half, full)

A load fraction is a cubic-yard count: the fraction times your truck. The example truck is 15 cubic yards, so a quarter load is 3.75, a half is 7.5 and a full load is 15. Run the formula once for each, with the hours, miles and special items that load actually takes. Time and dump trips do not grow in a straight line with volume. The $19 spreadsheet's price chart prints a starting price for each load size you list.

Dump fees and special items

Per ton, the dump line is weight × your fee. Per load, use the flat fee your dump charges instead. If the dump has a minimum per visit, use that when the per-ton amount would be lower. Mattresses, TVs, appliances and tires get their own fees, added on top, because they take extra handling or a separate disposal charge. The example uses $40 for a mattress and a TV together. You type every fee; nothing here looks up your local dump.

What the formula leaves to you

Pounds per cubic yard, dump fees, pay, overhead, the minimum charge and the target margin are your numbers, and they decide the price. The example's $145.98 profit is 35.2% of the $415.00 price, a bit above a 35% target because the floor is rounded up. That 35% is the calculator's default so the example matches; it is not a typical margin. Compare the result with prices you already know you can charge before you quote.

Questions

How do I price a junk removal job?

Add the dump fee, special-item fees, fuel, truck wear, labor and overhead. Price floor = (that cost + any card fixed fee) ÷ (1 − card fee % − target margin). Round the floor up to a price you would quote, and never go below your minimum charge. The made-up example on this page goes from $256.69 of cost to a $415.00 price. The free calculator does this for one job from numbers you type.

How do I price junk removal per cubic yard?

Price the whole job from its own costs first, then divide that price by the cubic yards on the truck. In the made-up example, $415.00 ÷ 7.5 cubic yards is $55.33 per cubic yard. Use that only as a check. A job with different weight, miles, crew time or dump fees needs its own cost total, not the previous job's rate per yard.

How do I price a job by truck load (quarter, half or full)?

Turn the fraction into cubic yards: quarter, half or full times the size of your truck, then run the same formula. The example truck is 15 cubic yards, so a quarter load is 3.75, a half is 7.5 and a full load is 15. Type the hours, miles, dump weight and special items that load actually takes. A larger load often means more time and sometimes another trip to the dump, so do not multiply the half-load price by two and call it a full load. On the spreadsheet, load sizes are an editable table, and loads bigger than the truck count as extra dump trips.

How do I include dump fees?

Cubic yards × your pounds per cubic yard gives the weight. Divide by 2,000 for tons, then multiply by what your dump charges per ton. The made-up example is 1,875 lb, shown as 0.94 tons, and the dump line is $79.69. If your dump charges a flat fee per load, or a minimum per visit, use whichever amount you will actually pay (the per-ton total, the flat fee or the minimum). The calculator on this page prices a per-ton fee. The spreadsheet also has a flat fee per load and a minimum per visit. It does not know what your local dump charges. Dump fees and weights differ by place, so you type your own rates. Every number in the example is made up.

How does a minimum charge work?

Set a minimum when a small job still costs you the trip. After the price floor is rounded up to your step, charge the rounded price or your minimum, whichever is higher. The made-up example rounds to $415.00, so a minimum at or below that does not change the quote. The free calculator's minimum box starts at $0 because this page does not print the example file's minimum. Type yours.

How do I price special items like mattresses, TVs and appliances?

Add a fee you choose for each item that costs extra to carry or to dispose of, on top of the load. The made-up example uses $40 together for a mattress and a flat-screen TV. Set the fee from the time and the disposal charge those items cost you, not from a published average. The spreadsheet has an item-fee table of 20 rows you can rename, including tires and appliances.

What margin should I target?

The target margin is the share of the price you want left after costs and the card fee. In the made-up example the profit is $145.98, which is 35.2% of the $415.00 price, a little over the floor because the price is rounded up. The free calculator starts at a 35% target so those figures match. That is not a typical or recommended margin. Raise it if you want more left after costs, or lower it to move the floor toward break-even, then compare the price with what you already charge.
